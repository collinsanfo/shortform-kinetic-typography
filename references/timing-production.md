# Timing and production

## Preserve the agreed material

Identify approved narration, wording, timeline, source footage, covers and logos. Hash files when byte preservation matters. Keep original assets separate from composites. A visual restyle generally reuses the voice and picture sources instead of triggering new scene, avatar or voice generations.

User-specific constraints such as browser choice, no cursor movement, palette, private pages, credit limits or a particular number of discourse markers belong in that project's brief. Do not make them universal requirements for every future user.

## Measure speech anchors

Use existing word times, cached local alignment, editor markers or a measured manual pass. Distinguish measured speech from intentional editorial leads. A phrase may appear just before its key noun, but record the lead so it is not later mistaken for measured synchronization.

Avoid repeatedly transcribing unchanged audio. Keep low-confidence recognition flags separate from confirmed defects. An ASR duplicate is not proof that an extra word was audibly spoken. A transcript is not listening review.

## Budget readable time

`readable hold = shot duration − entrance − exit/transition`

A fast entrance does not compensate for an unreadably brief hold. Around 2.5–4 familiar words per second can be a starting estimate; technical terms, perspective, names and competing focal points may need longer. This is an editorial heuristic, not a guaranteed accessibility or retention threshold.

Whole-word entrances around 0.12–0.25 seconds can suit energetic work. Longer movement is justified when it communicates a relationship or physical action. Give the words a clear settled state. Repeating the same bounce across every thought becomes distracting.

For long scenes, introduce a meaningful next visual state or thought. For short scenes, show the core subject immediately rather than waiting for an object entrance that occurs after the transition starts. Quiet moments can remain quiet.

## Narration rhythm

Preserve a good speaker's pace. Visual energy can come from phrasing, word entrances and picture changes without speeding up speech. Breaths and short silences help turns in meaning.

`So` can introduce a transition; `wait` can mark a genuine interruption. Use them where the story calls for them, not as supposedly proven conversion tricks. If the user limits their count, check the script and delivered speech. Do not impose one or two markers on every future project.

For synthesized speech, verify current supported pause syntax in official documentation. Unsupported tags may be spoken aloud. Keep generation input separate from the intended transcript. Do not change a saved voice to solve typography.

## Proof efficiently

Use real fonts and media in a few representative layouts. Include short and long phrases and an advanced treatment as needed. A short motion proof with real audio checks synchronization; a contact sheet only helps compare compositions.

Make routine corrections internally. Ask about creative preferences when a material decision remains unresolved, rather than imposing approval for every proof. Do not repeatedly export a whole film without a new issue to resolve.

## Export correctly

Use the requested aspect ratio and resolution. Upscaling does not create missing detail; report the actual source/export limits. Keep editable layers or a reproducible plan.

When exact audio preservation matters, compare decoded PCM in a lossless master with the canonical source. A sharing MP4 commonly uses lossy audio, so compressed byte equality is the wrong test. Avoid a shortest-stream mux that cuts the closing picture or last word. Inspect early frames, transitions, speech ending and final active frame. Distinguish an intentional closing hold from a blank tail.
