# Composition recipes

## Diagnose before choosing effects

| Reference feature | What to change |
|---|---|
| One word dominates | Enlarge the semantic word; reduce support |
| Broad heavy or tall condensed letterforms | Choose width and weight to fit the phrase and picture |
| Selective italic or colour | Emphasize meaning, not arbitrary alternate words |
| Type overlaps the person | Use actual background/type/subject layering |
| Type sits on a floor or wall | Derive a coherent perspective plane |
| Small setup and large payoff | Preserve their grammatical reading order |

With a still reference, proposed entrances are your design decisions. Do not claim to have observed a tracking method, motion speed, exact font or conversion result.

## Build a type system

Choose roles, not a different font for every shot. **Display** carries the main word; **support** completes the phrase; **accent** provides occasional italic, outline or weight contrast. Broad heavy type can feel open and emphatic; a condensed face can accommodate a tight vertical stack.

A display/support size ratio around 1.8–3.5 is a useful experiment, not a rule. Short support can tolerate a larger contrast; a calm brand may need less. Test at phone size. Prefer deliberate line breaks to stretching letters horizontally or shrinking the support until it disappears.

Use actual glyph metrics, including italic overhang, descenders and outline width. Render complete shaped words before masking or perspective transforms. Cropped character tiles can cut stems and lose kerning. Different sizes still need deliberate baselines and spacing.

## 1. Dominant word with quiet setup

Example: small `Make it`, large `CLEAR`. Align the setup with a meaningful edge of the payoff. The two must read as a phrase, not detached labels. The complete short phrase may appear immediately, or the main word can arrive on the spoken stress.

Use for one clear payoff. Avoid making an entire long question equally enormous. A restrained overshoot may suit playful material; impact does not require bouncing.

## 2. Offset stack

Example: `What comes` above an offset `NEXT?`. Compose two or three intentional lines. Offset should guide the eye instead of scattering it. Place the stack beside a face or object when that leaves better space.

Use for setup/payoff questions. Supporting words must remain legible after downscaling. Do not rotate every line merely to make it look edited.

## 3. Filled word and outline echo

Repeat one word decoratively behind or beside the filled primary word. Mark the echo as decorative in the plan so text-preservation checks do not mistake it for new content. There must still be one obvious primary reading.

Use sparingly for a strong beat. Thin outlines disappear under compression, while many echoes create noise over busy footage. An echo is not an excuse to duplicate the full sentence everywhere.

## 4. Subject occlusion

Composite background, large text, clean subject matte, then any foreground text. A shadow alone does not put the person in front of letters. Keep enough identifying letter shapes visible to recognize the word; hiding its central portion completely defeats the hierarchy.

Inspect hair, fingers, glasses and translucent edges at full size and during motion. A clean static cutout is not automatically a good video matte. Avoid text crossing the face unless specifically appropriate to the design. Do not reuse a reference creator's portrait or imply endorsement.

## 5. Surface perspective

Project type onto a floor, desk, page or wall with a four-corner transform. Derive the plane and vanishing direction from the visible surface. Tilting a flat title is a different effect from projecting it coherently onto a surface.

For moving footage, track the plane and inspect for sliding. Add foreground occlusion when objects pass in front. Match contrast, blur, lighting and any shadow without obscuring the words. Avoid putting essential text into an unreadable steep angle or beneath platform controls. A static proof establishes composition only, not tracking quality.

## 6. Text close to a gesture or object

Place setup words near a gesture and a larger payoff in adjacent open space. Connect the parts through alignment, scale and timing. For a hand pulling or pushing a word, align contact, movement and occlusion, then use restrained follow-through.

Literal interaction needs suitable footage and tracking. A simple adjacent phrase is better than an elaborate effect with visibly wrong contact. Do not claim a static cutout proves physical interaction.

## Contrast, variety and restraint

Choose a small palette that fits the brief. White over a dark area can work, but an intermittently bright moving wall may need a different position, local darkening or restrained shadow. A yellow or blue accent is useful only when legible and meaningful. Do not change locked artwork to match a reference palette.

Check the actual background over time. Numerical contrast on a flat swatch is an aid, not proof that moving type is readable. Avoid heavy glow that blurs the letterforms.

Uniform top headings above photographs remain a slide template even with faster camera motion. Vary composition because the thought or image warrants it. Keep enough shared typography, palette and rhythm that the film still feels coherent. Do not turn every pause into activity.
